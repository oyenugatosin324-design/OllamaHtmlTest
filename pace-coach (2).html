Pace Coach

Practice speaking and see your pace, pauses, tone and clarity. It tells you when to slow down, speed up and stop to breathe.

Your voice and your files are analysed inside this page. Nothing is sent anywhere. Recordings are kept in memory only until you leave the page.

How to read this page (start here)

Start in one minute

  1. Open Practice, press Start practicing and talk for 30 seconds about anything. Wait for the 3-2-1 first.
  2. Watch the big banner while you talk. It is the only thing you need to glance at.
  3. Press stop, then read the first thing under What to try next and do just that one thing in your next take.

The banner and the cues

  • Teal, "Good pace": your speed is inside the range you set.
  • Orange, "Slow down": you are above the top of your range. Finish the sentence, then stop for a count of two.
  • Amber, "Speed up a little": while you are actually talking you are below the bottom of your range. It ignores pauses, so a pause never counts as slow.
  • Blue, "Pause and breathe": you have gone past your breath limit without a stop. "Find a stopping point" is the early warning. "Pause" means you are already resting, which is right.
  • In Settings you can add soft sounds or a spoken voice to these cues, and change the countdown. Use headphones with sounds or voice, otherwise the microphone hears them.

Calibration: what it is and what it changes

Your computer cannot hear words here. It counts syllables, the beats in speech ("com-pu-ter" is three), and divides by an assumed number of syllables per word to estimate words per minute. Calibration measures how well your microphone and voice get counted. You read a short passage that has a known 91 syllables, and the page compares what it heard with 91.

  • How: open Set up below, press Start reading and read the passage in your normal voice. The level number should move when you talk. It stops by itself when you finish.
  • What changes: only the words-per-minute estimate (the syllables-per-word figure). It does not change how pauses, pitch or clarity are measured.
  • When to redo it: after you change microphone or room. It is optional. Without it the page assumes 1.35 syllables per word.
  • If it fails: it tells you why and changes nothing. You can record the passage on your phone and choose "Use a recording instead".

The timeline

Three lanes share one time axis, left to right.

  • Words per minute (top): the line is your pace over the last 10 seconds. The pale teal band is your range. The line turns orange where you are above it.
  • Pitch (middle): each dot is where your voice was, measured up and down from your own middle note. A flat row of dots is a flat voice; a row that rises and falls is a lively one.
  • Speech stripe (bottom): teal is you talking. Blue is a pause long enough to breathe (half a second or more). Orange marks where you talked past your breath limit.
  • While you practise it shows the last 60 seconds. After you stop it shows the whole take.

What each number means

  • Words per minute is an estimate, usually within 10 to 15 percent. Very fast speech, above about 7 syllables a second, is under-counted. Pauses are included, so pausing lowers it, which is the point.
  • Above your range is the share of time you spent faster than the top of your range.
  • Pauses are quiet gaps of a quarter second or more. A gap of half a second or more is long enough to breathe. Typical pause is the middle value.
  • Longest nonstop is the longest run of talking with no pause.
  • Tone variety is how far your pitch moves, in semitones (one piano key). Under 3.5 is narrow, 3.5 to 6 moderate, above 6 wide. A small study of English TED speakers found about 7.
  • Tone changes counts moments when pitch, loudness or speed settled at a clearly different level. It measures change, never feeling.
  • Volume range is the spread of your loudness. Signal is your voice against the room noise; under 20 dB means move the microphone closer.
  • Syllable dips is how deep the quiet gaps between syllables are. Crisp speech has deep dips; mumbling, echo and heavy compression make them shallow.
  • Held sounds are steady voiced stretches of 0.3 s or more, usually "uh" or "um", or a stretched word. Restarts are bursts of tiny fragments, like "I... I... the...".
  • Natural or even checks four signs of reading aloud without life: little pitch movement inside phrases, phrases that all end the same way, similar phrase lengths, and little stress on key syllables.
  • The score out of 100 adds five parts: pace 30, breathing and pauses 20, smooth flow 20, tone variety 15, clarity cues 15. It is a summary of these measures, not a judgement of your talk. Compare similar takes.

Listening back, marking and saving

  • After a take you can play it, loop all of it or just one section, slow it to 0.75x, and tap Mark this spot where you hear a mistake. Suggested spots (your fastest moment, held sounds) are listed too.
  • The recording lives in this page's memory only. It is gone when you reload. Download it first if you want to keep it; a strong take is highlighted.
  • The Progress tab keeps each session's score, measures and advice in this browser so you can see change over time. It never keeps audio.

Tone lab and drills

  • The page can only measure whether your pitch, loudness and speed moved. It cannot know what you felt or how a listener heard you.
  • "Hear it" uses a voice built into your browser, so what you hear depends on your device. The target numbers shown beside each tone are the real guide.

What none of this can do: understand your words, check your facts, hear which word you said, or know the emotion you meant. The cut-offs came from tests on generated speech and a handful of real recordings, so treat them as starting points and compare yourself with your own earlier sessions.

For the best readings

  • Use a quiet room and keep the microphone about a hand-span from your mouth.
  • Use Chrome, Edge or Firefox. If you open the file inside another app or a preview, the microphone is blocked.
